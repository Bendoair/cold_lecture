# Next features (deferred)

These are planned but not part of the first release. Each entry says **what** the feature would be for the student. It does not describe how to build it. Some already have a visible but inactive placeholder in the interface.

## Mindmap graph

A visual map of the student's dictionary: terms as nodes, linked concepts as connections. The student can pan, zoom and follow the threads they built up during the term, and spot isolated concepts that have nothing linked to them yet. It complements the dictionary list rather than replacing it, and opens from the *Mindmap* toggle in the top bar (with a *Split* view showing slides and map side by side). Every link made in the dictionary today will appear in it.

## Games

Short, deliberate break activities reachable from the **games** button in the bottom bar. They are meant to release focus pressure for a minute or two without leaving the lecture app entirely, and to end on their own so the student drifts back to the slide rather than away from it. Ideally they reuse what was just taught, for example a quick match-the-term round drawn from the dictionary.

## Messages

A lightweight messenger in the bottom bar (**msgs**) for classmates or the course group: quick questions like "what slide are we on?" or "did he say this is on the midterm?" without picking up a phone. The first version is a mock that gives the chrome a believable presence. It is not a full communication product.

## Study buddy

A small companion panel that reacts to the session. It can celebrate a long focus streak, notice when the student keeps leaving, suggest a short stretch break, and offer a one-line recall cue about the current slide or a recently used dictionary term (for example "Remember: Stokes' theorem equates the boundary flux with the interior curl"). Its value is gentle accountability and spaced recall. It is not a chatbot and does not replace the student's own notes. It can be collapsed to a quiet state.

## Interactivity meter

A visible gauge of how engaged the session has been: notes written, dictionary lookups and links made, todos captured, focus continuity, slides revisited. It gives the student honest feedback on their own participation during a lecture ("mostly listening" versus "actively working through it") and shows how that changes across lectures. Settings already store an interactivity checkpoint (default 50%) for when the meter appears; the meter UI itself is still deferred.

## Header and dead-space companions

Small decorative creatures (dogs, cats and similar), in the spirit of Google Colab's corgis and kittens, that idle in the header or other unused parts of the layout. They are pure low-stakes delight: they make long sessions feel friendlier, can be switched off in Settings, and never compete with the slide for attention. They are not a study tool. A first companion already appears in the header when Animals is enabled; richer creatures come later.

## Slide annotations

Drawing and highlighting directly on slides with mouse, pen or stylus. Annotations stay on their slide alongside that slide's note and can be shown or hidden. The pen button in the slide header is the placeholder for this.

## Cloud sync

The same notes, dictionary, todos and imported decks on every device the student uses (laptop in class, desktop at home, later a tablet). Today everything is stored only on the device it was created on.

## In-app Google search

A quick web lookup from inside the app with results in a popup, so checking a definition does not mean leaving fullscreen and losing the lecture. It would sit next to local search but stay clearly separate from it, because it goes online.

## Note autocomplete

Suggestions while typing notes: dictionary terms, symbols and phrases the student often uses, so notes keep up with a fast lecturer. Accepting a suggested term can also link the note to that dictionary entry.

## Android and iOS

Phone and tablet versions, with tablets as the main target for stylus annotations. On mobile, focus locking can rely on the platform's own tools (screen pinning on Android, Guided Access on iOS), which are stronger than what a browser can offer but need the student's consent.
